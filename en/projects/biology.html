On the Conjecture of Proteins as Genetic Material in Extremely, Extremely Special Organisms

Explores whether proteins can act as primitive genetic carriers, working from prion self-replication and reverse transcription, and treats the limits of the central dogma as the question at stake.

Question
Can proteins serve as genetic material in extremely unusual organisms, the way DNA and RNA do?
Method
Theoretical analysis. Tests whether the four classic approaches to identifying genetic material — isotope labelling, centrifugation, the subtraction principle and sequencing — still hold for retroviruses, and brings in hypotheses about the origins of proteins and viruses.
Conclusion
Proposes that reverse transcriptase acts as the genetic material of retroviruses only together with RNA, and suggests proteins may have played a genetic-material-like role during the transition from non-living to living systems. The author notes the question is still debated.

Molecular Biology Research on the Calvin Cycle in C3 Plants

Walks through the Calvin cycle, the core carbon-fixation pathway, from a molecular-biology angle: the reaction sequence is broken down step by step, key reactions are shown as molecular structures, and the route to sugar is analysed.

Question
How does each step of the Calvin cycle actually work, and where does the sugar come from?
Method
Literature review plus molecular structure diagrams. Reconstructs carbon assimilation in reaction order, draws the structural changes at each key step, then analyses how sugar is formed.
Output
Turns a complex assimilation pathway into a clear, referenceable sequence for later molecular-biology work. The manuscript is still being revised.

Analysis and Verification of Orange Plate Discoloration in Yeast Experiments

Investigates why culture plates occasionally turn orange during yeast cultivation, ruling out colour sources one by one through controlled-variable experiments, and uses the case to set out an approach to error analysis and lab quality control.

Observation
In a class experiment the yeast first grew normal milky-white colonies. Some time later the colonies were covered in orange until the whole plate had turned orange.
Method
First analyses and rules out four possible causes (changed growth conditions, mutation, metabolites and exudates, contamination by other microbes), then designs controlled-variable experiments: swapping yeast strains, separate dilution batches, large flask versus small beaker, sealed versus unsealed, streak plate versus spread plate.
Status
Colouring caused by the yeast's own metabolites has been ruled out — concentrations are too low and complexation cannot colour a whole plate. The contamination line of enquiry is still running and the manuscript is unfinished.
